Before the City wakes

The Night Bakers of Calle Alba

From nine at night until half past five, two people and a ninety-five-year-old oven make the bread a whole quarter will eat without a thought. This is what they think about instead.

The night shift, hour by hour

21:00

The key

No. 1 of 7

Nobody on Calle Alba can tell you when the bak­ery closes, only when it begins: at nine at night, when the bar­ber’s win­dow goes dark and Marta Ferrer turns a key that is older than she is. The shut­ter goes up with a sound like a dry cough. Inside, the room is cold and smells of last night’s oak, of scorched flour, of that min­eral note old brick keeps like a mem­ory. She does not switch on the big lights. She switches on the small one above the star­ter jar and looks at what is inside it the way you look at a sleep­ing child.

22:15

Flour, water, salt

No. 2 of 7

The star­ter has domed over­night, freck­led with bubbles, smell­ing of green apples and cel­lars. Tomás Ibáñez, who ar­rived at fif­teen to sweep the floor and never left, tips two sacks into the trough, the first of four batches: twen­ty-five kilos each, milled forty kilo­metres away by a man who still names his wheat by the field. Water goes in warm, salt last. The mix­er turns. Around minute six the shag­gy mass gives up and be­comes one glos­sy body, and Tomás lays a palm flat a­gainst it, the way you steady a horse, and nods. For Sun­day’s sweet buns he still beats the eggs by hand, in a chipped e­nam­el bowl, with a whisk whose han­dle has worn to the shape of his grip.

“Bread is the only thing we make that has to be finished before anybody can ask for it.”

Marta Ferrer, head baker
23:30

The fire

No. 3 of 7

Then comes the wait­ing, which is most of the job. The dough rests under lin­en while the oven is fed: oak splits stacked in a cross­hatch inside a hearth that has been fired every night since 1931, except two. Both nights are pen­cilled on the wall be­side the flue, one for the flood, one for Marta’s father. Tomás strikes a match. The first smoke leans toward the chim­ney like some­thing cu­ri­ous. For three hours the oven eats, and the room warms until the win­dows weep and the two of them work in shirt­sleeves in Oc­to­ber.

01:00

Shaping

No. 4 of 7

At one the dough is cut with a steel scrap­er into rounds the weight of a new­born, nine hun­dred grams each, checked on a brass scale nei­ther of them needs. Marta shapes with the flat of her hand, drag­ging each piece toward her across the bench until the skin tight­ens and squeaks. Each boule goes seam-up into a lin­en bas­ket dust­ed with rye. Four hun­dred and twen­ty times, under yel­low light, without a word. In twen­ty-six years they have nev­er once had to say behind you.

02:30

The hearth

No. 5 of 7

At half past two Tomás rakes the em­bers into a glow­ing drift at the back and swabs the floor with a wet cloth on a pole: a hiss, a white rush of steam, the bricks going from red to black. Marta holds her palm at the mouth and counts. At three she pulls away; that is two hun­dred and six­ty de­grees, and no ther­mom­e­ter has ever ar­gued. She scores each loaf with a ra­zor on a stick, one stroke, curved like the lip of a wave. Tomás cuts a cross into his, as his grand­moth­er did, to let the bread breathe or the dev­il out; he has nev­er de­cid­ed which. Then the peel whis­pers across the hearth, shh, and the loaf lands with a soft knock. Shh. Knock. Shh. Knock. It is the rhythm the whole street sleeps to with­out know­ing.

04:00

The singing

No. 6 of 7

Forty minutes on, the first loaves come out the col­our of strong tea and begin, faint­ly, to sing: a fine crack­ling as the crust con­tracts, like rain ar­riv­ing on a tin roof.

“That’s the sound of it being finished,” Marta says, and tilts her head at each one, lis­ten­ing. Tomás loads the cargo tri­cy­cle for the hos­pi­tal kitch­en, the café on the plaza, the school that will not open for hours. The street is blue and empty, ex­cept for a sweep­er, and a cat on the rim of the foun­tain who has, ap­par­ent­ly, also been up all night.

“People think we get up early. We don’t get up. We just haven’t gone to bed.”

Tomás Ibáñez, second baker
05:30

The city wakes

No. 7 of 7

At half past five the first tram shiv­ers past and Marta rolls up the shut­ter. The light that falls in is not sun­light yet, only the grey-blue idea of it. A nurse com­ing off nights buys a loaf too hot to slice and car­ries it a­gainst her chest like a hot-wa­ter bot­tle. A tax­i dri­ver takes two. By six the city will be up and hun­gry, and no­body will won­der where the bread came from, which is how Marta knows it went well. She hangs her a­pron, feeds the star­ter, and turns the old key. Then she walks home through a city that is on­ly be­gin­ning to smell of her work.

Take it home

Marta’s morning loaf

She has never written it down for the shop, but she wrote it down for us, on the back of a delivery slip, in pencil. Fold it open; it takes a day, most of it waiting.

Recipe No. 7 · Horno Ferrer, Calle Alba 14

Pan de Alba

One large boule · about 24 hours, mostly waiting

ApprovedM.F.in pencil

You will need

  • Active starter90 g
  • Strong white flour450 g
  • Wholemeal or rye flour50 g
  • Water, just warm360 g
  • Fine sea salt10 g
  • Rye flour, for the basketa handful

What to do

  1. EveningWhisk the starter into the water, add both flours, and leave it alone for forty minutes. Then work in the salt with wet hands.
  2. Every half hourFold the dough over itself, gently, four times. It should end up slack, alive and just short of doubled.
  3. BedtimeShape it tight, seam-up, into a floured basket. Cover it and let the refrigerator take the night shift, ten to fourteen hours.
  4. MorningHeat a lidded iron pot for forty-five minutes at 250 °C. Turn the loaf out, score it once, decisively, and lower it in.
  5. Twenty, then twenty-fiveBake twenty minutes with the lid on and twenty-five with it off, until the crust is the colour of strong tea.
  6. Then listenCool it on a rack and hold your ear close. Wait an hour before slicing. Marta insists.

Salt the water, not the starter. And never speak ill of the starter within earshot.— M.F., on the back of a delivery slip
